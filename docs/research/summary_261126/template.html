Summary

  • Densities: close, partly by cancellation. All ${n_el} 1 M electrolyte estimates at 20 °C lie between ${el_lo} and ${el_hi} % of the measured fit of [1], which is itself known to ${q_lf_err}. The pure solvents are off by more: the open-chain carbonates of the open force field (M0) are up to ${m0_max_dense} % too dense, its EC ${m0_ec_light} % too light; the OPLS-AA EC of model M1 is right (${ec_m1_dev} %).
  • Conductivity: both models too low, by a factor of ${k_ratio_lo} to ${k_ratio_pilot}. Measured κ at 1 M, 20 °C is ${k_exp} S/m [1]. The pilot (M0) gives κ a factor ${k_ratio_pilot} lower. M1, the current model, gives κNE a factor ${k_ratio_m1} lower, and κNE ignores the ion correlations that lower κ further. No model run meets the ±15 % criterion. One probe of the parameter scan, ${best_name}, reaches κNE = ${best_k} S/m (${best_frac} %, ${best_in_band} the band): one run, κNE rather than κ, and a parameter changed only to map trends. It points at EMC (task SC-7), it is not a model.
  • The known biases point the same way. The 1 fs step reads the density ${q_sc1_estimate} (estimate from SC-1); the liquid also runs ${q_t_el} K warmer than displayed. Both make the simulated ions faster, so correcting them widens the κ gap.
  • Transference number: no test yet. t₊ in the solvent frame is ${tp_sol} against ${tp_lf} measured [1]; the error is as large as the value.
  • Not compared yet: self-diffusion (no measured value for this electrolyte in our ledger), viscosity of the electrolyte, κ(T), the κ maximum, the thermodynamic factor. Nothing has been fitted to transport data.
  • Against the literature (section 6). The direction of every error agrees with what others report for fixed-charge models: ions too slow, linear carbonates too dense. We disagree with Lehnert's model [2] on κ and on the Li⁺ shell. Possibly new: the usual 1 M density check passes by cancellation. The ρ(c) slope of the standard scaled-charge ion model is ${sc4_slope_def} % too small, Lehnert's published boxes imply the same, and the charge scale ζ cannot repair it. That suggests a second cause for the 3 M κ excess they report, which can be tested.

Not found in the literature searched: candidates for new results

  1. The 1 M density check passes by cancellation. The straight line through the model's points meets c = 0 ${rho0_off} % above the fit of [1]: the solvent is too dense, like its EMC. Each mole of salt then adds ${sc4_slope_def} % too little density. The two cancel near 1 M (${sc4_1} %), and 3 M is ${sc4_3} %. Lehnert's boxes imply a similar slope, ${leh_slope} against ${lf_slope} [1], and ${leh_3} % at 3 M (Fig. 3). The two models share the ion model (JJ06 Li⁺, CL&P PF₆⁻, ζ = 0.75), not the solvent parameters, so the slope belongs to the ions. [5] shows ρ(c) only as a figure, states agreement "especially at salt concentration >0.5 M", and does not discuss the slope. Not found: a carbonate paper that tests a scaled-charge model's ρ(c) over 0.1–3 M against experiment with numbers.
  2. The charge scale cannot fix the salt volume. dVφ/dζ = ${dvz_1} cm³/mol at 1 M but ${dvz_3} at 3 M. Reaching the measured Vφ would need ζ ≈ ${zstar_1} at 1 M but ${zstar_3} at 3 M, beyond full charge (linear extrapolation, an estimate). So the excess is in ion size and packing, not only in missing electrostriction. This fits [5]: with ion charges that its text implies are unscaled, its densities agree from 0.5 M on. The physics is known in water: scaled charges with the LJ of full-charge ions leave the ions too large, the reason for the ECC-R σ reduction [10] and for refitting the ion LJ to solution densities in the Madrid-2019 model [9]. We found it shown for no carbonate.
  3. A second explanation for the 3 M κ excess. Lehnert write: "the MD simulations overestimate the conductivity for LiPF6 in EC:EMC and in EC:DMC:PC at c = 3.0 M. This could originate from using a constant scaling factor ζ" [2]. Their 3 M box is ${leh_3} % too light by the arithmetic above, and in both our scan and [7] lighter boxes have faster ions. Part of the excess may be the volume error, not the screening. Testable (a below).
  4. A sensitivity map of transport. ln κNE against ln ζ has exponent ${q_scan_zeta_exp}, against the EMC ε ${q_scan_eps_exp}; Li⁺ σ is not resolved (section 2; 25 November summary). Not found: a published sensitivity of κ to the solvent LJ parameters of a carbonate.
  5. The time-step bias of the common protocol. At 1 fs with flexible bonds (the step of [2]) the density reads ${q_sc1_estimate} against 0.5 fs (SC-1). That time-step errors exist is known in general; a number for this electrolyte was not found.

What we could simulate that would be new

  1. 3 M at the measured density. κ of the present model at its own density and at the density of [1] (the question of trial T3, at 3 M). If κ falls back toward experiment at the measured density, the volume error explains the excess; if not, the screening does. This needs the existing model and long transport runs at 3 M.
  2. The same ions in other solvents. Density over c of the ion model in pure EMC and in PC against [11] and [12]. There the measured Vφ rises steeply with c in EMC and hardly at all in PC. This shows whether the volume error depends on the solvent. Density runs only, cheap.
  3. The Madrid-2019 recipe for a carbonate. Charges from the electronic continuum correction (ECC) plus a few pair LJ terms (PF₆⁻ F, Li⁺–O carbonyl) fitted to ρ(c,T) in two or three solvents and to the Li⁺ shell, with κ, D, t₊ and η as predictions only. Not found for carbonates. The nearest are polarizable force fields [6] and the density alignment of a machine-learned potential [13]. The engine and spec 0.1.2 already carry pair terms.
  4. κ(c,T) and the κ maximum predicted, not fitted. Lehnert fit ζ to κ [2]. A model fitted only to non-transport data that places the κ maximum within ±0.2 M (decision 7) would be the result of the paper. In its absence a measured failure is a result too.
  5. The thermodynamic factor of the electrolyte against the measured one [1], after the NaCl known-answer test (section 4).

None of a–e chooses a parameter from κ, D or t₊. Physical parameters and pass criteria for b and c still need the author's approval.
